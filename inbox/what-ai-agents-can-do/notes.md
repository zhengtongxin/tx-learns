## Goal
Know what an agent is, what jobs it suits, and what it still cannot do, so you can spot real uses at your own company.

## Points
1. An agent is a model plus a loop: it reads a goal, picks a tool (search, spreadsheet, email, database), looks at the result and decides the next step until done or stuck.
2. Good fits: jobs with many small steps, a clear end, text or data as input, and a cheap way to check the result (answer a supplier email, reconcile invoices, draft a reorder list).
3. Poor fits: jobs where one wrong move is costly and nobody checks, jobs with no written rules, jobs that need real-world judgement or empathy.
4. Three levels to tell apart: a chatbot answers, a workflow follows steps you fixed, an agent chooses its own steps. More freedom means more value and more risk.
5. Ask of any idea: what is the input, what is the output, who checks it, what does a mistake cost?

## What I got wrong (common slip-ups; tell them through Mdm Rohani or Mr Tan)
- Thinking 'agent' means a robot that replaces a person. Most useful agents take over one boring step of a job.
- Picking the flashiest job first instead of the one with an easy check.
- Calling a plain chatbot an agent in a board paper.

## Try it yourself
List five jobs in your own workplace. For each, write input, output, checker and cost of a mistake. Mark the one that scores best.

## Takeaways
An agent is worth trying where steps are many, the end is clear and a check is cheap. Start with one boring step, not a whole job.
