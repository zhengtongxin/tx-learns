## Goal
Understand the job: an engineer who works inside the customer's business, turns a messy request into a working AI system, and stays until people actually use it.

## Build order
1. Picture Kaya Travel Cover: a small insurer on a shophouse street. Their claims team works a queue by hand: read the story, check the policy, approve, decline or refer.
2. The FDE's first move is not code. It is sitting next to Aisha for an afternoon and watching what she really does.
3. Write down the job in one paragraph: what goes in, what comes out, who signs it off, what a mistake costs.
4. Three skills mixed together: engineering (build it), consulting (find the real problem), and trust (people must be willing to use it).
5. The path I follow in this track: pilot, pick a model, give it a harness, measure it, add memory, make it safe to pause, improve it, secure it, hand it over.

## Typical outcome pattern
Most of the early value comes from asking better questions and writing a clear brief, not from clever code. A customer who says 'use AI on claims' usually means 'stop my team drowning in the same twenty kinds of claim'.

## What I got wrong
I thought the job was mostly coding. The hard part is agreeing what 'good' means with the customer before building: Mr Goh counts accuracy, Aisha counts fairness, Kumar counts risk.

## Try it yourself
1. Pick a process in your own life or work and write the one-paragraph job description for an AI helper.
2. List who would sign off its output and what a wrong answer costs.
3. Write three questions you would ask before building anything.

## Takeaways
An FDE builds inside the customer's world. Start with the process and its owners. Define success and the cost of mistakes first. Code comes after the brief.
